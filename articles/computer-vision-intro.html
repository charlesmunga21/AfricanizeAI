Introduction to Computer Vision

Computer vision is the field of teaching computers to extract meaning from images and video — going from raw pixel grids to information like "there is a car here" or "this is a picture of a maize field under drought stress."

An image, to a computer

A digital image is just a grid of numbers. A grayscale image is a 2D array of pixel intensities (0–255); a color image adds three such arrays, one each for red, green, and blue. A computer vision model never "sees" a picture the way a person does — it operates purely on this array of numbers.

# A tiny 3x3 grayscale image, as a computer would represent it
image = [
    [0,   128, 255],
    [64,  200, 90],
    [255, 30,  10],
]

Core tasks in computer vision

  • Image classification — assign a label to an entire image ("cat" vs. "dog").
  • Object detection — find where objects are, drawing bounding boxes and labeling each one.
  • Semantic segmentation — classify every individual pixel, useful when you need exact object boundaries (e.g. separating crop from soil in a farm photo).
  • Object tracking — follow an identified object across video frames over time.
Detection and segmentation are easier to understand once you've watched them run. Real-Time Detection points your camera at the world and draws YOLO boxes and MobileSAM masks live, on-device — nothing here is a screenshot or a canned example.

Why convolutional layers matter

Most modern vision systems are built on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Instead of connecting every pixel to every neuron (which would be enormous and ignore spatial structure), a convolution slides a small filter across the image, learning to detect local patterns — edges, textures, corners — which get combined into more complex shapes in deeper layers.

import torch.nn as nn

model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Conv2d(in_channels=3, out_channels=16, kernel_size=3, padding=1),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Conv2d(16, 32, kernel_size=3, padding=1),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Flatten(),
    nn.Linear(32 * 8 * 8, 10)   # e.g. 10 output classes
)
You rarely train a CNN from scratch today. Pretrained backbones (ResNet, EfficientNet, ViT) already know general visual features from millions of images — you fine-tune them on your specific dataset instead, which needs far less data and compute.

Where it shows up in practice

Computer vision powers crop health monitoring from drone or satellite imagery, quality inspection on manufacturing lines, medical image analysis (X-rays, retinal scans), license plate recognition, and mobile document scanning apps — among many other applications covered in the AI for Business tutorial.
