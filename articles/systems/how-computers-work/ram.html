RAM

RAM (Random Access Memory) is where a program's working data lives while it runs. Picture it as a desk: roomy enough to spread out everything you're currently doing, and fast to reach — nowhere near as fast as the CPU's own cache notepad, but leagues faster than walking to a filing cabinet across the room. Every open app, every tab, every piece of data currently "in use" on your computer is sitting in RAM right now.

Two things worth knowing about RAM

  • It's limited. A desk only has so much surface area. A computer might have 8, 16, or 32 gigabytes of RAM — once it's full, something has to give.
  • It's volatile. The moment power is cut, everything on the desk is swept onto the floor. RAM needs continuous electricity to hold its contents — that's the trade-off for being so fast.

This is exactly why your computer nags you to "save" your work: whatever's only in RAM doesn't survive a crash or a shutdown. Only once it's written to disk is it actually safe.

What happens when RAM fills up

When you open a new app and there's no free space left, the operating system has to make room — usually by closing or "swapping out" whatever hasn't been used in a while. That's the real reason a computer with too many tabs and apps open starts to feel sluggish: it's constantly shuffling things in and out of a desk that's too small for everything you're asking it to hold at once.
