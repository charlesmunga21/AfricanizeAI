Disk

If RAM is the desk, disk is the filing cabinet across the room — an SSD (solid-state drive) or an older HDD (spinning hard disk). It's where everything that needs to outlive a restart actually lives: your files, your installed apps, and every database on the machine. Walking to the filing cabinet takes longer than reaching across your own desk — but nothing on it disappears when the lights go out.

Why disk is so much slower than RAM

Going back to the scaled numbers from the CPU Cache submodule: if a CPU cache reference takes 1 second, a RAM reference takes a few minutes — but reading from an SSD stretches out to roughly six days, and a seek on an older spinning disk stretches to roughly ten months. PlanetScale's engineering blog puts the everyday version of that number simply: reading a page of data from RAM is "roughly 1,000 times faster" than reading it from even a fast modern SSD.

Why the huge gap? RAM is electronic circuitry built to be read and written near-instantly. An SSD has to route the request through flash memory chips designed for durability and cost per gigabyte, not raw speed. A spinning HDD is slower still, because there's an actual mechanical arm that has to physically move to the right spot on a spinning platter before it can read anything — that physical motion is what those extra milliseconds are spent on.

The trade-off, in one sentence

Disk is slower than RAM because it's built to be large, cheap, and permanent instead of fast — and that's exactly the job it needs to do. You wouldn't want your family photos to vanish the moment you unplug your laptop.

Visual model: power off, and see what survives

Open a program (goes to RAM) and save a file (goes to disk), then power the computer off. Watch which one survives.

This exact RAM-vs-disk trade-off is the entire reason database engines are engineered so carefully — they have to give you disk's permanence, at something closer to RAM's speed. That's the whole subject of the next, final submodule.
