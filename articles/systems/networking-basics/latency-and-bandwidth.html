Latency & Bandwidth

"The internet feels slow" can actually mean two completely different things, and it's worth being able to tell them apart:

  • Latency is the delay — how long it takes for the very first bit of a reply to come back after you ask for something.
  • Bandwidth is the capacity — once data is flowing, how much of it can move per second.

Two everyday analogies

Latency is like calling someone on the phone: the delay before they pick up, no matter how much you plan to say afterward. Bandwidth is like the number of lanes on a highway: more lanes let more cars move per minute, but adding lanes doesn't make any single car arrive faster once it's driving. A connection can have huge bandwidth and still feel laggy for a video call, or modest bandwidth and still feel snappy for simple browsing — they're genuinely separate things.

Why distance matters so much

A big part of latency, especially for long distances, is simply physics: nothing — not even a signal down a fiber-optic cable — can travel faster than the speed of light. Round-tripping to a server on the other side of the planet takes real, physical time no amount of clever engineering can fully erase. You already saw this kind of gap once, comparing CPU cache, RAM, and disk. Network distance turns out to belong on that exact same scale.

Visual model: the full picture, from CPU cache to another continent

This extends the scaled-latency comparison from the CPU Cache submodule with two more rows — a round trip within the same data center, and a round trip to another continent. Notice where they land.

Look closely and something surprising jumps out: a round trip to another machine in the same data center can be faster than a single seek on a spinning hard disk. Distance across a network, when it's short, doesn't have to be the slow part — physical movement inside a mechanical disk can be slower than a trip "outside the box" altogether.
