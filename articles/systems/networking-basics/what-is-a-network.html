What Is a Network?

A network is nothing more mysterious than two or more devices that can send messages to each other. Your phone talking to your laptop over Wi-Fi is a network. Your whole house — phone, laptop, smart TV, all sharing one Wi-Fi router — is a network. And the internet is the same idea, just enormous: it's what you get when huge numbers of separately-run networks all agree to pass each other's messages along.

The internet isn't one thing — it's an agreement

Nobody owns "the internet." Your internet provider runs its own network. A university runs its own network. A company running a big website runs its own network too. What makes it all "the internet" is that these networks agree on common rules for passing messages to each other, so a message from your phone can hop across several different companies' equipment and still arrive at the right place, on the other side of the world, correctly. That's genuinely the whole idea — everything else in this module is detail on how that handoff actually happens.

Most of what you do online is "ask, then answer"

Almost everything you do on a network follows the same shape: your device (the client) asks a question, and some other computer that's always on and waiting (the server) answers it. Loading a web page, opening an app, watching a video — all of it is a client asking a server for something.

You've actually already met this exact pattern. In the Databases submodule, Postgres runs as its own server that a client application connects to. It's the same client-server relationship — the only difference here is that the "connection" is happening across a network instead of on one machine.
