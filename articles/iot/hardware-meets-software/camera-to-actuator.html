A Camera, an AI Model, and an Actuator

This is your third example from the start of this module — "a camera can be connected to a computer and work" — and there's a genuinely well-documented real system built entirely around it. Blue River Technology, a Stanford-founded startup acquired by John Deere in 2017 for $305 million, builds See & Spray: a machine that's pulled through a field and decides, plant by plant, whether to spray it.

The pipeline, at machine speed

As the machine moves through the field — modern systems run at up to 16 mph — cameras (as many as 36 on the largest systems) continuously capture the crop row passing beneath. Each frame is classified in real time: crop, or weed. There's no time to send that frame to the cloud and wait for an answer — the model runs directly on the machine, an approach called edge computing, precisely because the round-trip latency of a network call (remember the Latency & Bandwidth submodule) would be far too slow for a decision that has to happen dozens of times a second. Deere reports the system performing plant-level classification, decision-making, and actuation in milliseconds, processing images upward of 20 times a second.

The last step is exactly the actuator pattern from earlier in this module: the classification decision becomes a GPIO write, and a nozzle actuator fires — or doesn't — on that exact spot. Deere reports growers cutting non-residual herbicide use by nearly half using this system.

Every module in this section, in one machine

  • A camera and a CPU running a model — Computer Vision and How Computers Work.
  • A decision made on-device instead of over a slow network round trip — Networking Basics.
  • An embedded control loop reading a classification and writing an actuator — this module's Sensors & Actuators.
  • A whole computer, running an OS, coordinating cameras, a model, and actuators all at once — Operating Systems Basics.
